# Effects of the Amendment 5 gear configuration requirements on species interaction rates in the American Samoa longline fishery: a depth-based hazard analysis

## Management-relevant outcomes

The Amendment 5 gear requirements moved hooks below the depths where green sea turtles, oceanic whitetip sharks, mahimahi, and wahoo are most often caught; at unchanged depth-specific rates, this would reduce turtle interactions per set by about half. Observed turtle interactions declined by less than this and the decline could not be distinguished from no change, so the realized benefit is uncertain. Removing the requirements is projected to increase turtle interactions per set by about 1.6 times, and by up to 2.2 times if vessels add hooks and shorten float lines, with comparable or larger increases for the other shallow-dwelling species and a 12–16% reduction in albacore catch per hook. These projections are robust to assumptions about gear shoaling but rest on 49 observed turtle interactions and estimated, rather than measured, hook depths.

**Keywords:** green sea turtle bycatch; longline hook depth; gear configuration regulation; depth-specific hazard model; American Samoa longline fishery

# Executive summary

**Purpose.** Amendment 5 to the Pelagics FEP, effective in September 2011, requires American Samoa longline vessels longer than 40 ft fishing south of the Equator to configure their gear so that baited hooks fish deeper than about 100 m. The requirements include at least 70 m of mainline without branch lines on each side of every float and float lines at least 30 m long, and were intended to reduce interactions with green sea turtles. As the Council reviews these requirements, this report addresses two questions: how interaction rates changed after the requirements took effect, and how much of that change is attributable to deeper hooks; and how interaction rates would change if the requirements were removed.

**Approach.** The analysis used fishery observer data from 7,622 sets (3,137 before and 4,485 after the regulation) for five species: green sea turtle, oceanic whitetip shark, mahimahi, wahoo, and albacore. The depth of every hook was estimated from the recorded gear configuration, with and without an adjustment for current-driven shoaling. For each species, the interaction rate per hook was estimated as a function of hook depth, with additional terms for regulatory period and for variation among trips and years. Because the depth-specific rate is assumed to be the same in both periods, the analysis can separate the effect of moving hooks deeper from other changes in interaction rates.

**Interaction risk depends strongly on hook depth.** Interaction rates for green sea turtle, oceanic whitetip shark, mahimahi, and wahoo were highest on the shallowest hooks and declined with depth. Albacore showed the opposite pattern, with the lowest rates on the shallowest hooks and the highest between about 175 and 250 m. The regulated gear configuration eliminated hooks shallower than about 85–110 m, depending on the shoaling assumption.

**The gear change was large enough to reduce turtle interactions substantially.** If interaction rates at each depth had stayed the same, the regulated gear would produce about 46% fewer green sea turtle interactions per set than the pre-regulation gear (95% interval 30–59%). The corresponding reductions were 62% for mahimahi, 38% for oceanic whitetip shark, and 30% for wahoo, while albacore catch per set would increase by 15% (Table ES-1).

**Observed changes were smaller than the gear change alone would predict for turtles, sharks, and mahimahi.** Observed green sea turtle interactions fell from 0.73 to 0.58 per 100 sets, a 21% decline that is not statistically distinguishable from no change given 23 and 26 observed interactions. In the model, the gap between the expected and observed reductions corresponds to a higher turtle interaction rate at a given depth after the regulation, but this period effect is poorly determined (1.56; 95% interval 0.73–3.33). The data are therefore consistent with the gear change having reduced turtle exposure as intended, with the benefit partly offset by other changes or masked by the small number of interactions. Mahimahi interactions per set declined by 27%, less than half the 62% reduction expected from the gear change. The observed 46% decline in wahoo was largely explained by deeper hooks, whereas oceanic whitetip shark interactions increased by 83% despite a gear change that would have reduced them, indicating that factors other than hook depth dominated the change for that species. The analysis cannot identify those factors.

**Removing the requirements would increase interactions with shallow-dwelling species.** If the blank mainline were removed and vessels kept the same number of hooks per basket, expected green sea turtle interactions per set would increase by a factor of 1.6 (95% interval 1.3–2.1). If vessels used the freed mainline to set additional hooks (about 21% more hooks per set), the increase would be 2.0 times, and 2.2 times if float lines were also shortened to the pre-regulation median of 25.5 m. Returning to pre-2011 gear would increase interactions by 1.9 times. In absolute terms, the turtle interaction rate would rise from about 0.6 to 0.9–1.2 per 100 sets. Interactions would increase by 2.1–3.1 times for mahimahi, 1.4–1.9 times for oceanic whitetip shark, and 1.3–1.7 times for wahoo. Albacore catch per hook would decline by 12–16%; catch per set would fall by 12% if hook numbers were unchanged but rise by 3–6% if vessels added hooks. These results changed by less than 3% between the two depth models.

**Limitations.** Hook depths are estimated from gear geometry rather than measured, and for many sets they rely on a filled value for mainline length. The reported intervals reflect uncertainty in the estimated depth relationships only and understate the full uncertainty. The turtle results rest on 49 interactions. The period effects describe associations with the post-2011 period, not their causes, and the projections assume that the depth-specific interaction rates observed in the regulated period continue.

# Introduction

The American Samoa longline (ASLL) fishery is a federally managed commercial fishery operating primarily in the South Pacific Ocean and targeting albacore tuna (*Thunnus alalunga*) for the tuna canning industry in Pago Pago. The fishery also catches other pelagic species, including yellowfin tuna (*Thunnus albacares*), bigeye tuna (*Thunnus obesus*), swordfish (*Xiphias gladius*), and various shark species. Fishing operations typically use deep-set pelagic longline gear consisting of a suspended mainline, float lines, and baited branch lines deployed at varying depths throughout the water column. Although the fishery primarily targets tuna, incidental interactions with protected species, including sea turtles and sharks, have raised conservation concerns and prompted the implementation of mitigation measures intended to reduce bycatch while maintaining the economic viability of the fishery.

In response to concerns regarding interactions with Pacific green sea turtles (*Chelonia mydas*), the Western Pacific Regional Fishery Management Council (Council) developed Amendment 5 to the Fishery Ecosystem Plan for Pelagic Fisheries of the Western Pacific Region (Pelagics FEP). The amendment was intended to reduce interactions between the ASLL fishery and green sea turtles by modifying longline gear configurations to increase the minimum fishing depth of baited hooks. Analyses supporting the amendment indicated that green sea turtle interactions occurred disproportionately on hooks deployed in the upper 100 m of the water column, particularly those positioned closest to the floats \[citation\]. Because green sea turtles generally occupy relatively shallow waters, increasing hook deployment depth was expected to reduce the spatial overlap between fishing gear and sea turtle habitat, thereby decreasing the likelihood of incidental capture.

Amendment 5 was implemented through a final rule published on August 24, 2011 (76 FR 52888), which became effective on September 23, 2011. The regulations apply to U.S. longline vessels greater than 40 ft (12.2 m) in length fishing south of the Equator. The rule established specific gear configuration requirements designed to position baited hooks deeper than 100 m, including: (1) float lines at least 30 m in length; (2) a minimum of 15 branch lines between consecutive float lines; (3) branch lines at least 10 m in length; and (4) a minimum distance of 70 m along the mainline between each float line and the nearest branch line. The minimum branch-line length requirement was incorporated from the 2010 Endangered Species Act (ESA) Section 7 biological opinion. Additionally, the rule prohibited the possession or landing of more than 10 swordfish during a single fishing trip, with the intention of discouraging shallow-set fishing practices that could increase sea turtle interactions. Collectively, these requirements were intended to shift fishing effort toward deeper portions of the water column and reduce the risk of interactions with green sea turtles and potentially other protected species.

Although Amendment 5 was implemented to reduce sea turtle interactions, its effectiveness has not been comprehensively quantified using an analysis that explicitly accounts for fishing depth and changes in fishing effort. Evaluating the effectiveness of the regulation is complicated by the relatively low frequency of observed sea turtle interactions, variability in fishing operations, and changes in the distribution of fishing effort over time. Furthermore, changes in fishing depth may affect the capture probabilities of other species, including target and nontarget fish and protected sharks, whose vertical distributions and habitat preferences differ from those of green sea turtles. Consequently, an evaluation of Amendment 5 should consider both changes in species interaction rates and changes in the depth distribution of fishing effort associated with implementation of the regulation.

More recently, the Council has expressed interest in reassessing the ASLL gear configuration requirements as part of a broader review of existing fishery regulations. This review includes consideration of the conservation benefits of the measures, their operational and enforcement implications, and their potential effects on target and nontarget species. A quantitative evaluation of observed interaction rates before and after implementation of Amendment 5 can provide information relevant to determining whether the measures have achieved their intended conservation objectives and identifying potential consequences of modifying or removing the existing requirements.

This report has two objectives. The first is retrospective: to evaluate how species interaction rates changed following implementation of Amendment 5 and to separate the part of that change attributable to the redistribution of hooks across depths from changes in interaction rates at a given depth. The second is prospective: to project how interaction rates would change if the gear configuration requirements were removed, under alternative assumptions about how the fleet would reconfigure its gear. Both analyses use fishery observer data and estimated hook depths to characterize the depth distribution of fishing effort and species interactions before and after implementation of the regulation.

Species-specific interaction rates are estimated with a model in which the expected number of interactions per hook depends on the estimated depth of the hook, with additional terms for regulatory period and for variation among trips and years, and with the number of hooks fished as the measure of effort. The depth-specific interaction rate per hook (hereafter the depth hazard) is assumed to be the same in both periods, so the gear requirements affect interaction rates through the depth distribution of hooks, while the period term captures differences in interaction rates at a given depth that are not explained by hook depth. This distinction is important because an overall reduction in interactions may result from moving hooks into depths where a species is less frequently encountered, even when the underlying depth-specific hazard remains unchanged. Conversely, changes in fishing practices, environmental conditions, or species availability may contribute to differences in interaction rates independent of the regulatory gear configuration requirements. The analysis therefore provides a quantitative assessment of associations between Amendment 5 implementation, fishing depth, and species interactions, while recognizing the limitations of attributing observed changes exclusively to the regulation.

The results are intended to inform the Council's review of the ASLL gear configuration requirements by characterizing how fishing depth influences interaction risk for target, nontarget, and protected species, by assessing the extent to which changes in observed interaction rates following implementation were associated with the change in hook depth, and by estimating the change in interaction rates expected if the requirements were removed.

# Methods

## Data

We used fishery observer records from the ASLL fishery for \[2006–final year\]. Each record corresponds to one animal caught and includes trip and set identifiers, set-level gear characteristics (reported mainline length, float line and branch line lengths, number of floats, hooks per float, and hooks per set), and the hook position of capture within the basket. The analysis was restricted to five species: albacore, wahoo (*Acanthocybium solandri*), oceanic whitetip shark (*Carcharhinus longimanus*), mahimahi (*Coryphaena hippurus*), and green sea turtle. Sets were assigned to the pre-regulation period (no blank mainline) or the regulated period (70 m blank mainline at each float) according to whether the haul ended before or after 23 September 2011. After screening (below), the data comprised 7,622 sets: 3,137 pre-regulation sets from 109 trips and 4,485 regulated sets from 154 trips (Table 1).

## Hook depth estimation

Hook depths were estimated with the catenary equation of Yoshihara (1951), modified to accommodate the blank mainline. The modification places the regulated basket's hooks on the same catenary curve as an unregulated basket of equal length by treating the blank sections as occupied by virtual hooks at the operational hook spacing. For a basket of mainline length 2*L* with *N* hooks and blank length *L*<sub>b</sub> at each float, the hook spacing is

$$
S = \frac{2L - 2L_{b}}{N + 1}
$$

the number of virtual hooks in each blank section (*N*<sub>b</sub>), the virtual position of physical hook *i* (*j*), and the total number of virtual hooks on the curve (*N*<sub>v</sub>) are

$$
N_{b} = \frac{L_{b}}{S},\quad j = i + N_{b},\quad N_{v} = N + 2N_{b}
$$

so that hook *i* lies at a distance *L*<sub>b</sub> + *iS* from the float. The depth of hook *i* is

$$
D_{i} = d_{f} + d_{a} + L\left\lbrack \sqrt{1 + \cot^{2}\varphi} - \sqrt{\left( 1 - \frac{2j}{N_{v} + 1} \right)^{2} + \cot^{2}\varphi} \right\rbrack
$$

where *d*<sub>f</sub> is the float line length, *d*<sub>a</sub> is the branch line length, and *φ* is the angle between the horizontal and the mainline at the float. Setting *L*<sub>b</sub> = 0 recovers the standard Yoshihara equation. Basket length was calculated as reported mainline length divided by the number of baskets, with the number of baskets taken as reported hooks per set divided by hooks per float. A catenary angle of 68° was assumed for all sets.

Catenary equations overestimate realized fishing depth because currents, vertical shear, and wind shoal the mainline. In the Hawaii-based deep-set fishery, Bigelow et al. (2006) found that tuna sets monitored with temperature-depth recorders reached on average 79–86% of their predicted maximum depth (depending on how the sag ratio was estimated), and summarized deep-set gear as reaching about 70% of predicted depth. Boggs (1992), as summarized by Bigelow et al. (2006), reported observed depths averaging 54% and 68% of predicted depths in two years of experimental tuna sets near Hawaii. Depths were therefore computed under two depth models: unadjusted catenary depths, and shoaled depths in which the catenary sag term was scaled so that the deepest (apex) hook reached 70% of its predicted depth. With *d*<sub>0</sub> = *d*<sub>f</sub> + *d*<sub>a</sub> and apex sag *L* tan(*φ*/2), the sag scaling factor for a target multiplier *m* is

$$
s = \frac{m\left( d_{0} + L\tan\frac{\varphi}{2} \right) - d_{0}}{L\tan\frac{\varphi}{2}}
$$

applied to the sag term of every hook in the basket. Under this formulation hooks adjacent to the float are unaffected, shoaling increases toward the apex, and hook order within the basket is preserved. Because temperature-depth recorder studies have measured only the deepest hook, the distribution of shoaling along the basket is an assumption.

## Data screening

Of 7,668 observed sets, 104 were flagged for implausible or inconsistent gear and 46 were excluded (22 pre-regulation and 24 regulated). Sets were excluded when the basket length was outside 200–2,000 m (14 sets) or the predicted unshoaled apex depth exceeded 500 m (32 sets). Twenty-two of the excluded sets had a reported mainline length of exactly 73,149 m, and many had low reported hook counts (median 740 hooks per set among sets excluded for basket length), producing baskets of up to 8 km and predicted depths of up to 2,800 m. The value 73,149 m is also the median mainline length across all sets, which indicates that it was used to fill missing mainline lengths for a substantial number of sets; hook depths for those sets therefore depend on a filled value (\[n\] retained sets, \[x\]%). A further 58 sets in which the basket count implied by reported hooks differed by more than 15% from that implied by floats were retained, because basket length was calculated from reported hooks (the median ratio of the two basket counts across all sets was 0.97). All recorded hook positions fell within the reported number of hooks per basket, so no catches were excluded.

## Effort and catch by hook position

Fishing effort was reconstructed from set-level gear rather than from catch records, so that hooks that caught nothing contributed to effort. For each set, every hook position 1, …, *N* was assigned the number of baskets in the set as its hook count and an estimated depth from the equations above. Catches of each species were tallied by set and hook position and joined to the effort table, with zeros for positions without catch.

## Depth hazard model

For each species and depth model, catch at hook position *k* in set *j* of trip *t* in year *y* was modeled with a generalized additive model,

$$
\log\mu_{jk} = \log h_{jk} + \beta_{0} + \beta_{r(j)} + f\left( D_{jk} \right) + u_{t} + v_{y}
$$

where *h*<sub>jk</sub> is the number of hooks at that position (an offset), *β*<sub>r</sub> is a fixed effect for regulatory period, *f* is a smooth function of hook depth (the depth hazard on the log scale), and *u*<sub>t</sub> and *v*<sub>y</sub> are random effects for trip and year. Counts were modeled as negative binomial for albacore, wahoo, oceanic whitetip shark, and mahimahi, and as Poisson for green sea turtle given the small number of interactions. The depth smooth used a thin-plate regression spline with basis dimension 8 (4 for green sea turtle). Models were fit with the bam function in the R package mgcv (Wood 2017) using fast restricted maximum likelihood and discretized covariates (Wood et al. 2017). Soak time, hook type, bait, and location were not included.

The depth function *f* was shared between periods. The regulation changed where hooks were set, not the vertical distribution of the species, so the effect of the gear change enters the model through the depth distribution of hooks. The period effect *β*<sub>r</sub> absorbs differences between periods in the interaction rate at a given depth, and the trip and year effects absorb variation among trips and years. Pre-regulation sets supply the information on interaction rates at hook depths shallower than about 100 m, so the depth function is estimated over the range of depths that would be fished if the requirements were removed.

## Decomposition of observed changes after implementation

The observed change in interactions per set from the pre-regulation to the regulated period was expressed as the product of two components. The gear (depth) component is the ratio of expected interactions per set with regulated-period gear to that with pre-regulation gear, calculated with the same depth hazard; it measures the change expected from the change in hook depths (and hooks per set) alone. The period component is exp(*β*<sub>r</sub>), the multiplicative change in the interaction rate at a given depth between periods. Their product is the model-implied change, which was compared with the observed ratio of interactions per set. Intervals for the observed ratio were obtained by resampling trips within each period (2,000 bootstrap samples); intervals for the gear component used 2,000 draws of the depth-smooth coefficients from their approximate multivariate normal posterior distribution; and intervals for the model-implied change combined those draws with normal draws of *β*<sub>r</sub>.

Scenarios S2 and S3 represent fleet responses in which the freed mainline is used to set additional hooks, with S3 also allowing float lines shorter than the 30 m minimum. ## Projection, interaction rates, and uncertainty

For each scenario, expected catch was calculated as

$$
E = \sum_{k}^{}{h_{k}\exp\left( f\left( D_{k} \right) \right)}
$$

summed over all hook positions in all sets, with hook depths rounded to 1 m for computation. The intercept, period, trip, and year terms are common to all scenarios and cancel in comparisons, so scenarios were compared as ratios to the base case: the ratio of expected interactions per set, which includes the effect of additional hooks in S2 and S3, and the ratio per hook, which isolates the change in depth distribution. Uncertainty was characterized by 2,000 draws of the depth-smooth coefficients; reported intervals are the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. Interaction rates under each scenario were calculated as the observed interaction rate per set in the regulated-period sets multiplied by the scenario ratio, with uncertainty in the observed rate from a trip-level bootstrap combined draw by draw with the depth-smooth draws. Scenario rates therefore describe the regulated-period fishery, with its abundance, fishing grounds, and practices, fished with the scenario gear.

# Results

## Observed interactions

Albacore dominated the catch of the five species, and green sea turtle interactions were rare, with 23 interactions in the pre-regulation period and 26 in the regulated period (Table 1). Observed interactions per set were lower in the regulated period for albacore, wahoo, mahimahi, and green sea turtle, and higher for oceanic whitetip shark.

## Depth-specific interaction rates

The estimated depth functions separate the species into two groups (Figure 1). Interaction rates for mahimahi, oceanic whitetip shark, wahoo, and green sea turtle were highest on the shallowest hooks and declined with depth. Mahimahi declined most steeply, falling to a quarter or less of the maximum rate by 150 m. Oceanic whitetip shark and wahoo declined more gradually. For green sea turtle, the smooth reduced to a log-linear decline with depth (effective degrees of freedom 1.0), and the trip random effect was estimated as negligible; the confidence band is wide, reflecting the 49 interactions. Albacore showed the opposite pattern: rates were lowest on the shallowest hooks, rose to a plateau between about 175 and 250 m, and remained high at greater depths. The shape of each function was similar under the catenary and shoaled depth models, with the shoaled curves shifted toward shallower depths, as expected when all hook depths are compressed.

The blank mainline requirement removes all hooks shallower than about 110 m under the catenary model and about 85 m under the shoaled model (Figure 2). The fitted depth range was 44–500 m under the catenary model and 39–350 m under the shoaled model. The remainder of the depth distribution changes little, because the requirement affects only the hooks nearest the floats.

## Model fit

Model estimates are summarized in Appendix A. The depth smooth was significant for every species under both depth models (p \< 0.001; Table A1). Its effective degrees of freedom ranged from 4.7 to 6.8 for the four fish species, indicating nonlinear depth relationships, and was 1.0 for green sea turtle, so the turtle model reduced to a log-linear decline in interaction rate with depth (χ<sup>2</sup> = 23.7 under the catenary model). Trip and year random effects were significant for all four fish species, indicating substantial variation in catch rates among trips and years beyond that explained by depth. For green sea turtle, neither the trip effect (edf \< 0.01, p = 0.61) nor the year effect (p = 0.11) was significant, so turtle interactions showed no detectable clustering by trip or year once depth was accounted for. The period effects are given in Table A2 and discussed with the decomposition below.

The negative binomial shape parameter *θ* was 2.0–2.6 for albacore, wahoo, and oceanic whitetip shark and 0.89 for mahimahi, the most overdispersed species, consistent with aggregated catches (Table A3). Deviance explained ranged from 11% (oceanic whitetip shark) to 34% (mahimahi) for the fish species and was 4.7% for green sea turtle. The low value for turtle reflects 49 interactions spread over 231,708 set × hook-position cells, nearly all of which had none; it indicates that individual turtle interactions are not predictable at the scale of a single hook position, not that depth is a weak predictor of where they occur. Estimates under the shoaled depth model were nearly identical to those under the catenary model (Tables A1–A3).

Observed and fitted catches by 20 m depth bin are shown in Figure 3 and tabulated in Tables A4 (catenary) and A5 (shoaled). Because the models include the intercept, period term, and trip and year random effects, fitted totals closely match observed totals for each species and period (exactly for the Poisson model for green sea turtle and within about 2% for the negative binomial models), so fit is assessed mainly by how catches are distributed across depth bins. Fitted values generally tracked observed values in both periods, with three departures relevant to interpretation. First, catches in the shallowest regulated-period bins exceeded fitted values for green sea turtle (7 interactions observed against 5.6 fitted at 120–140 m under the catenary model, and 8 against 4.9 under the shoaled model) and for mahimahi (111 against 67 at 100–120 m and 841 against 666 at 120–140 m). Second, the model overpredicted pre-regulation albacore catch at 200–240 m (about 15,200 observed against 18,100–18,500 fitted in each bin). Third, the green sea turtle data were sparse: pre-regulation bins between 100 and 260 m held 0–3 interactions each, and three of them held none. These departures are concentrated in the shallow part of the regulated-period distribution and indicate that a single depth function with a period intercept does not capture all differences between periods.

## Changes in interaction rates following implementation

Observed interactions per set declined after implementation for albacore (ratio 0.81; 95% interval 0.73–0.90) and wahoo (0.54; 0.49–0.59). The declines for mahimahi (0.73; 0.49–1.07) and green sea turtle (0.79; 0.46–1.43) had intervals that included one, and interactions per set with oceanic whitetip shark increased (1.83; 1.45–2.34) (Table 3).

The gear component isolates the effect of the change in gear on interaction rates. It was calculated by applying the same fitted depth hazard to two sets of hooks: the pre-regulation sets as fished and the regulated-period sets as fished. Because the depth hazard is identical in both calculations, any difference in expected interactions per set arises only from differences in how the gear was configured, chiefly the depths at which hooks were fished, and not from changes in abundance, fishing grounds, or other conditions between periods. The component therefore answers the question: if interaction rates at each depth had stayed the same, how much would interactions per set have changed because the fleet changed its gear? It reflects all differences between the two periods' gear, not only the 70 m blank mainline: the longer float lines required in the regulated period (the pre-regulation median was 25.5 m), changes in basket length and hooks per basket, and a small difference in hooks per set (pre-regulation sets averaged about 3% more hooks). It is the reciprocal of the per-set ratio for scenario S4 in Table 4 (for green sea turtle, 1/1.86 = 0.54).

For green sea turtle, the gear component was 0.54 (95% interval 0.41–0.70): with depth-specific interaction rates held constant, the regulated gear would produce about 46% fewer interactions per set than the pre-regulation gear. The corresponding reductions were 62% for mahimahi (0.38; 0.36–0.40), 38% for oceanic whitetip shark (0.62; 0.57–0.66), and 30% for wahoo (0.70; 0.69–0.71). The size of each reduction follows from the shape of the species' depth hazard (Figure 1) and the hooks that the regulation moved (Figure 2). The regulation removed hooks from depths shallower than about 110 m, where the hazards for these four species are highest, and placed them deeper, where the hazards are lower. The reduction is largest for mahimahi, whose hazard declines most steeply over the upper 150 m, and smallest for wahoo, whose hazard declines more gradually. Albacore shows the opposite effect: its hazard is lowest on the shallowest hooks and highest between about 175 and 250 m, so moving hooks deeper increased expected albacore catch per set by 15% (1.15; 1.15–1.15). The intervals reflect only uncertainty in the estimated depth hazard; they are narrow for the abundant species because their depth hazards are estimated from large numbers of catches, and wide for green sea turtle, whose hazard is based on 49 interactions.

The period component differed among species. For wahoo it was close to one (0.95; 0.83–1.09), so the observed decline in wahoo is largely accounted for by the deeper hooks. For albacore the period component was below one (0.81; 0.76–0.86): albacore rates at a given depth were lower in the regulated period, partly offsetting the gain from deeper hooks. For mahimahi (1.60; 1.14–2.24), oceanic whitetip shark (1.59; 1.02–2.47), and green sea turtle (1.56; 0.73–3.33), rates at a given depth were higher in the regulated period, offsetting part or all of the reduction expected from the gear change. For green sea turtle this component is poorly determined, and its interval includes one.

The model-implied changes were broadly consistent with the observed changes for green sea turtle (0.84 against 0.79 observed) and mahimahi (0.61 against 0.73), but less so for wahoo (0.67 against 0.54), albacore (0.93 against 0.81), and oceanic whitetip shark (0.98 against 1.83). The period component is estimated after accounting for variation among years and trips; because years are nested within periods, part of the difference between periods is attributed to the year effects, and the model-implied change need not reproduce the observed change. The large discrepancy for oceanic whitetip shark indicates that much of its observed increase is associated with particular years or trips in the regulated period rather than with a uniform shift between periods.

## Projected effects of removing the gear requirements

Removing the blank mainline increased projected interactions for all four shallow-associated species and decreased projected albacore catch per hook (Tables 4 and 5). For green sea turtle, removing the blank mainline with the same number of hooks (S1) increased expected interactions per set by a factor of 1.61 (95% interval 1.28–2.05) under the catenary model and 1.59 (1.27–2.03) under the shoaled model. Using the freed mainline to set additional hooks (S2) increased the factor to 2.00 (1.58–2.58), and also shortening float lines to the pre-regulation median (S3) increased it to 2.15 (1.65–2.85). Returning to pre-regulation gear (S4) gave 1.86 (1.42–2.46). All coefficient draws produced ratios greater than one in every scenario.

Among the other species, mahimahi showed the largest increases (2.1–3.1 times per set across scenarios), followed by oceanic whitetip shark (1.4–1.9 times) and wahoo (1.3–1.7 times). Albacore catch per hook declined by about 12–16% across scenarios, because the added shallow hooks fish at depths where albacore rates are low. In S2 and S3 the additional hooks offset this decline, so albacore catch per set increased slightly (1.03–1.06 times). The per-set and per-hook ratios are identical for S1, which does not change the number of hooks.

## Sensitivity to depth assumptions

The projected ratios and decomposition components were nearly identical under the catenary and shoaled depth models; for every species and scenario the two estimates differed by less than 3%. Uniform shoaling compresses all hook depths, and the estimated depth functions shift with them, so the depth model changes the absolute depth scale of the functions but not the relative change in risk. The results are therefore robust to the magnitude of shoaling, provided shoaling is similar under regulated and unregulated gear. A test in which shoaling differs between configurations, and a sensitivity to the catenary angle (Bigelow et al. (2006) reported median angles of 60–64° for Hawaii deep-set gear, shallower than the 68° assumed here), would establish whether this holds when depth errors differ between configurations.

# Discussion

The gear configuration required by Amendment 5 moved hooks out of the upper 85–110 m of the water column, depending on the depth model, and the estimated depth hazards indicate that this change alone would reduce interactions per set by about half for green sea turtle and by 30–62% for oceanic whitetip shark, wahoo, and mahimahi, while increasing albacore catch per set. In that sense, the regulation altered fishing depth in the direction and magnitude needed to reduce interactions with shallow-dwelling species.

The observed changes after implementation were smaller than the gear component for green sea turtle, mahimahi, and oceanic whitetip shark. For green sea turtle, the observed reduction of 21% was not statistically distinguishable from no change, and in the model this corresponds to a higher interaction rate at a given depth in the regulated period (period component 1.56). That component is poorly determined (0.73–3.33), so the data are consistent both with the gear change having reduced turtle exposure as intended while other factors raised the depth-specific rate, and with the realized benefit being masked by sampling variability given 23 and 26 observed interactions. The analysis cannot identify which factors underlie the period components; candidates include changes in species abundance, in the spatial or seasonal distribution of fishing, in fleet composition, and in observer coverage or recording, none of which are represented in the model.

Removing the requirements would increase projected green sea turtle interactions by roughly 1.6 to 2.2 times per set, with the larger values applying if the fleet used the freed mainline to set additional hooks or shortened float lines. Interactions with oceanic whitetip shark, mahimahi, and wahoo would increase by comparable or larger factors, and albacore catch per hook would decline. The choice of headline scenario should reflect the expected fleet response: S1 isolates the effect of the blank mainline alone, while S2 and S3 represent likely responses if fishers seek to maintain or increase hook numbers. Multiplying the projected rates by the fleet's annual fishing effort would give expected annual interactions under each scenario for comparison with incidental take statements.

## Limitations

These results rest on several assumptions. First, the projections assume that depth-specific interaction rates estimated from the observed record apply in the future; changes in green sea turtle abundance, in the spatial distribution of fishing, or in other gear characteristics such as hook type and bait would alter absolute interaction rates, although not necessarily the ratios. Second, hook depths are model estimates based on a fixed catenary angle and an assumed shoaling profile, without direct depth measurements for this fleet, and for a substantial number of sets they depend on a filled mainline length. Third, the depth function is shared between periods, with a period intercept; the model departures in the shallowest regulated-period hooks indicate that this structure does not capture all differences between periods, and a model with a period-specific depth function or a separate effect for the hooks nearest the float would test whether those departures affect the results. Fourth, the reported intervals for the removal scenarios reflect uncertainty in the estimated depth function (and, for the interaction rates, in the observed baseline rate), conditional on the assumed gear geometry and depth model; they do not include uncertainty in gear geometry, set-to-set variation in depth, or model structure, and are therefore narrower than the full uncertainty, particularly for the abundant species. Fifth, the green sea turtle depth function is based on 49 interactions and reduced to a log-linear decline; comparison with simpler formulations, such as a step at 100 m, and with the observed distribution of interactions by hook position, would show whether the projected turtle ratios depend on the form of the depth function. Finally, the period components are confounded with any other changes between 2006–2011 and the regulated period, so they describe associations rather than causes.
